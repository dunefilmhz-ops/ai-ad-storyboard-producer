# Dialogue, Performance and Camera Grammar

Use this reference for dialogue-led live action, vertical drama, emotional exchanges, action coverage and any story split across multiple model generations. Adapt it to the current style and model; do not force live-action rules onto animation or gameplay.

## Script fact lock

When faithful adaptation is required, preserve exact dialogue, event order, action owner, prop interaction and action result. Visual enrichment may add anticipation, breathing, gaze, micro-expression, reaction, environmental motion, sound cues and editorial inserts, but must not add dialogue, characters, conflicts or outcomes.

## Dialogue timing

Use spoken audio when available. Otherwise estimate Mandarin dialogue from a baseline near 4 characters per second, then adjust by performance:

- urgent or angry: roughly 4.5–5.5 characters per second;
- normal: roughly 3.8–4.5;
- sad, weak, intimate or hesitant: roughly 2–3.5.

Add about 0.2–0.5 seconds for breath, reaction or edit handles when the performance needs it. These are planning ranges, not a replacement for measured voice duration.

## Long dialogue coverage

Do not hold a long speech on one unchanged face. Preserve the audio while varying picture through:

- speaker relationship shot;
- listener reaction;
- over-the-shoulder angle;
- hand, prop or environmental detail;
- return to the speaker for the decisive phrase.

Use L-cut or J-cut when it improves pace and clarity. Label off-screen continuation in the editorial plan; do not ask the video model to invent lip sync for an unseen speaker.

## Observable performance

Translate emotion into a small, coherent set of visible signals:

- eyes and gaze behavior;
- brows, jaw, lips and breathing;
- one hand behavior;
- torso, shoulders and weight distribution;
- gaze target and reaction timing.

Choose two or three compatible signals. Avoid stacking every stereotypical symptom, numerical claims about teeth or pupils, or stage-like overacting unless the approved style requires exaggeration.

## Action coverage

For a large interaction such as a shove, hit, collision, fall or forceful prop exchange, consider three editorial beats:

1. relationship shot: cause, geography and initiating subject;
2. contact/detail shot: hand, prop or impact point when readable and safe;
3. consequence/reaction shot: affected body, emotion and result.

These may be separate generations or edit shots. Do not split small actions such as a glance, turn, sitting down or picking up a simple object unless the insert adds story information or rhythm.

## Camera motivation

Choose movement for narrative function:

- slow push: realization, intimacy or approaching threat;
- pull back or rise: isolation, aftermath or scale reveal;
- over-the-shoulder: relationship and dialogue geography;
- lateral track or follow: movement through space;
- focus transfer: connect foreground evidence to background reaction;
- handheld: instability, urgency or physical conflict;
- crash zoom, whip pan, Dutch angle or orbit: only for an approved impact or tonal beat.

Do not add movement merely because a prompt template contains it. Prefer one primary camera idea per generation.

## Cut continuity

Adjacent shots should change angle or shot size enough to read as a deliberate cut; use the 30-degree rule as a practical warning, not an inflexible law. Preserve screen direction, eyeline, prop hand, body orientation and movement vector. Carry an outgoing action into the next incoming shot when matching on action.

## Physical handoff checkpoint

At the end of every sliced generation, record:

- last shot size, angle, lens feel and camera height;
- each character's frame position, facing, gaze and pose;
- current action and next motion tendency;
- held props, contact points and object locations;
- key environment landmarks;
- light direction, color and exposure state.

The following generation must inherit this checkpoint unless a visible cut, time jump or scene change authorizes a reset.

## Vertical framing

For 9:16 delivery, protect headroom, hands, feet when action requires them, subtitles, CTA and UI safe areas. Avoid extreme facial crops when identity stability is at risk. Do not impose one-face-only composition universally; use the fewest clear faces the model can reliably maintain for the required relationship.
