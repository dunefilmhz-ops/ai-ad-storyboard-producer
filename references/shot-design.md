# Shot Design and Splitting

## Narrative units versus generation units

A client shot defines meaning. A production shot defines what one generation can reliably perform. Keep client numbering for audit, then assign production IDs such as `C03-A`, `C03-B` or `G07`.

## Split when

- the text contains two or more decisive verbs;
- the setting, time, costume state or camera axis changes;
- a reveal and the reaction both need to read;
- an action lacks a visible result;
- close emotion and multi-character geography compete in one framing;
- a transformation contains trigger, process and final pose;
- UI/exact text is mixed with character animation;
- the generation would otherwise exceed model duration or reliability.

## Add 1–2 short shots when

- a 0.4–1.2 second reaction improves emotional clarity;
- a 0.3–0.8 second insert clarifies a prop, reward, hit or clue;
- a 0.5–1.5 second result shot proves victory, failure or transformation;
- a scale contrast improves impact: close-up to wide, ground to aerial;
- a bridge makes a hard transition causal: impact flash to UI, smoke burst to new form.

Do not add a shot that repeats information, delays the hook, introduces new lore or exists only for empty camera movement.

## Advertising rhythm

- Establish the hook within the first 1–3 seconds.
- Alternate shot scale or information; avoid consecutive shots with nearly identical background and framing.
- Show action results, not only preparation.
- Use fast cuts at impacts and reveals; allow brief holds for emotion, selling points and CTA readability.
- Keep the core selling point from being delayed by decorative setup.

## Action timing heuristic

Estimate separately:

- anticipation: 0.3–1.0s;
- core action: 0.5–2.5s;
- impact/result: 0.3–1.2s;
- reaction/readability hold: 0.4–1.5s.

Generate extra handles before and after the intended edit. Complex multi-character actions generally need 4–8 seconds of generation even when the edit uses 1.5–3.5 seconds.

## Continuity grammar

Track left/right screen position, facing, movement vector, gaze, contact point, prop count, light source and exit direction. When reverse shots look too similar, vary angle and shot size while preserving geography.

For action continuity, the outgoing motion must motivate the next incoming motion. If a character walks out, show the exit; if a door opens next, establish the kick/push; if an effect comes from inside, do not move its source outside.
